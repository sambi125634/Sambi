# Choosing and running a funnel type

## Core principle: match the funnel to the sales team, not the trend

The single most important input to funnel choice is the sales team's
actual strengths and weaknesses, not what's currently popular. A team
that's only good at closing pre-warmed, easy "layup" deals will struggle
badly if dropped into a funnel type that requires live education/
objection handling from a cold state, and vice versa — moving a funnel
that succeeds on organic traffic (where trust is pre-built) directly onto
cold paid traffic without checking whether the sales team can still
educate cold prospects is a common, expensive mistake.

## The funnel archetypes

**Webinar** — condenses the trust-building of organic content into a
single event. Typical pattern: a short promotion burn (often ~3 days for
a live one) that runs at a loss, a revenue spike the day of/after, then a
tail that fades over roughly 7-14 days as call show rates decline and
sales-team "cleanup" follow-up captures the rest. Healthy expectation is
roughly 3-5x return; a struggling one is often near breakeven rather than
a clear loss. It's a lump-sum, relatively infrequent funnel (weekly at
best before automating) — before committing a test budget, sanity-check
that the budget is actually large enough to produce a meaningful payout
(see [[paid-funnel-testing-benchmarks]]'s pre-flight math check);
otherwise a business can stay stuck in an underfunded "test phase"
indefinitely.

**Call funnel** — best suited when the sales team can actually run
education-heavy calls, and works especially well fed by organic traffic
(where trust is pre-built) rather than cold paid traffic. A common
mistake is assuming the landing page/VSL does the educating — in
practice, front-end video engagement is often shallow ("scanner mode":
people are qualifying whether to book, not absorbing the pitch), and the
real education happens *after* booking, while the prospect is deciding
whether to actually show up ("justification mode"). This is why a
formal **back-end selling system** matters — five components used
between booking and the call: (1) short "breakout" videos placed on the
confirmation page for immediate consumption, (2) a value-dense email
sequence repeating the core framing for people who won't watch video,
(3) aggressive retargeting with a frequency target (a rule of thumb is
15-20 total content touches, mixing short- and long-form, before the
call), (4) setters who start the education process by phone/message
before the call itself so the closer isn't starting cold, and (5)
managing what AI assistants and search results say about the business,
since prospects increasingly research a company by asking an AI model
rather than searching manually. Treat this five-part system as
non-negotiable infrastructure for any call funnel, not an optional
add-on — it measurably improves show rate, close rate, and cycle length.
Call funnels are a strong fit for daily-lead-flow scaling (see the
two-lever model in [[paid-funnel-testing-benchmarks]]) because, unlike a
lump-sum webinar, they generate bookable opportunities every day.

**Low-ticket-to-high-ticket** — adds sales-cycle length by design, and
that's fine only if the economics of the low-ticket step can tolerate it.
Two key techniques: (1) build the low-ticket offer by extracting and
repackaging a slice of an existing high-ticket offer's content/material
rather than creating something new from scratch; (2) deliberately
compress the buyer's time to a felt "aha moment" (the point they feel
they've actually gotten value) — for slow-to-consume formats (e.g. a
book), pair the purchase with fast-digest formats (chapter-by-chapter
video/audio summaries, a sequenced email series) so the buyer's
perceived value lands in days, not months, since people ascend to the
high-ticket offer only after feeling they got value from the low-ticket
step, never immediately upon purchase. **Tolerance rule**: a long sales
cycle from low-to-high-ticket is only acceptable if the low-ticket offer
itself is roughly profitable or breakeven on its own; a low-ticket funnel
that's both unprofitable *and* slow to convert upward is a double
failure and should be fixed or dropped rather than treated as a
loss-leader indefinitely.

**DM-based / organic-DM funnels** — high-effort (frequent daily posting
with a comment-to-automation-to-human-setter handoff) and structurally
capped in most cases, for two compounding reasons: setter-level sales
talent is typically the weakest tier available, creating a ceiling on
team size/quality that's hard to push past, and attribution/tracking on
these funnels is usually poor, which makes owners reluctant to scale
spend confidently even when the channel is genuinely working (a
"scaling blind" risk-tolerance problem, distinct from but related to the
psychological retreat pattern in [[founder-growth-blockers-diagnostic]]).
Most operations running this model plateau in the low hundreds of
thousands per month; pushing meaningfully past that is rare and usually
requires accepting real platform risk (account restriction/ban exposure)
in exchange for scale.

**Challenge / multi-day event funnels** — the largest lump-sum funnel
type, and the least frequent (realistically annual, semi-annual, or
quarterly at most, given the effort to produce one). Expect a
significant burn period (commonly 60-90 days of promotion that's
unprofitable or barely breakeven) before a large payoff event. Best used
as an occasional, high-effort "cleanup" mechanism to convert an
accumulated pool of fence-sitters who haven't bought through the regular
daily/weekly funnels — not as a recurring core engine. Needing an
existing audience/brand of reasonable size makes the burn period more
tolerable; without one, the burn risk is much higher.

**Organic content as its own funnel type** — distinguished by an
unusually long sales cycle (often 3-6 months) but a strong compounding
and trust-building effect. Its main value is often as a background
multiplier for every other funnel: it acts as a "sales nurture asset"
that builds trust ahead of a paid-funnel encounter and gives a
follow-up/retargeting content library to lean on — worth starting early
regardless of which paid funnel is the primary revenue driver, precisely
because its payoff compounds slowly rather than converting instantly.

## The chunk method: combine funnels instead of over-pushing one

Most businesses scale primarily through one dominant funnel type at a
time, pushed to its real limit. But hitting a specific larger target
(a "next million," for example) sometimes genuinely requires deliberately
combining two separate funnel-driven revenue streams rather than
continuing to push a single funnel type past a scale ceiling it has
already reached (see [[meta-ads-scaling-mechanics]] on scale ceilings and
the scaling trough). Recognizing when you're in "one funnel, keep
amplifying" mode versus "need to chunk two sources together" mode is a
deliberate strategic call, not something to stumble into by accident.

## Picking one: bandwidth and bottleneck stack

Every funnel type has its own stack of bottleneck stages to test and fix
(traffic cost/CTR through to close rate and AOV, with the specific
intermediate steps — opt-in/retention for webinars, application/booking
steps for call funnels, post-purchase ascension steps for low-ticket).
Choosing a funnel type is also choosing how much testing bandwidth a team
needs to have to work through that entire stack — stacking multiple new,
unproven funnel types at once (see [[paid-funnel-testing-benchmarks]]'s
"run one new growth bet at a time") multiplies that bandwidth requirement
and usually backfires. Pick the funnel type that best matches both the
sales team's actual strengths and the team's current capacity to
methodically test and fix each stage of that specific funnel.
