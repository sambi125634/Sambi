# Multi-channel organic content system

## Map each content slot to a funnel stage, not just a topic

Rather than treating every piece of content the same way, assign each
recurring content slot a distinct funnel-stage purpose:

- **Bottom-of-funnel (single-topic depth)**: one instructor-style piece
  per topic, going deep on one lesson, carrying most of your calls-to-
  action — this is the format easiest to naturally weave a CTA into,
  since it's a direct address to the viewer.
- **Mid-funnel (demonstration/breadth)**: a format that shows the lessons
  being *applied* (a live consult, a case walkthrough, an interview)
  rather than explained — covers more lessons per piece but each at much
  shallower depth; CTAs are harder to weave in naturally here and should
  appear more sparingly.
- **Top-of-funnel (discovery/reach)**: a dedicated slot optimized purely
  for reach and new-viewer discovery, with a soft or no hard sales CTA —
  its job is expanding the top of the funnel, not converting, and it's
  the right place to experiment with formats aimed at a broader audience
  than your core paid-offer buyer.

## Split channels by audience/content-type, not just by platform

Distribution algorithms tend to route content toward viewers who already
watch that *type* of content — mixing formats (long-form authority
content, quick clips, casual personal content) on one channel can dilute
how well any single type gets distributed. Splitting into separate,
purpose-specific channels (one for core long-form authority content, one
for short-form/clips, one for a different audience or personal-brand
angle) lets each channel's algorithm-driven distribution specialize
around a consistent content type and viewer intent, rather than
competing signals diluting all of them. Deliberately keep a channel
aimed at a specific high-value audience free of content types (e.g.
short-form clips) that primarily attract a different, lower-value
audience, if diluting that channel's audience composition would hurt its
usefulness for your actual buyer.

## Calibrate content depth to what you're actually selling

How much of your real knowledge to give away for free should be a
deliberate function of the underlying offer, not a fixed habit:

- **Selling an education/info product**: deliberately withhold a
  significant fraction of full depth in free content (a common target is
  giving away roughly 10-20% of full depth) — revealing everything for
  free directly cannibalizes the paid product's reason to exist.
- **Selling a done-for-you service/agency offer**: reveal close to
  everything, including the full mechanism, since demonstrating deep
  expertise doesn't compete with the offer the same way — most buyers of
  a service don't want to do the work themselves even after seeing
  exactly how it's done; showing depth builds trust that converts into
  hiring you to execute it.

Decide which mode applies before setting a "how much to give away" policy
— applying the wrong mode (revealing everything while selling
information, or over-withholding while selling a service) works against
the underlying business model.

## Treat consistency as infrastructure, not a nice-to-have

Commit to a fixed publishing schedule and treat any missed drop as a
real cost to audience trust and habit formation — the same way a
recurring TV show missing its slot damages viewer expectations. This
matters more early on, when view counts are discouragingly low and it's
tempting to treat consistency as optional until "it's working" — the
audience that does show up early is disproportionately made up of people
already far along in a buying decision, so maintaining the cadence
matters even when the raw numbers look unimpressive.

## Never suppress calls-to-action to chase view count

Common organic-growth advice says to minimize CTAs to maximize reach and
watch time. If you have genuine conviction that your paid offer benefits
the viewer more than continuing to consume free content, treat frequent,
naturally-woven CTAs (not abrupt or salesy) as the responsible choice,
not a growth tax — a viewer who never hears the CTA never gets the
chance to get more value from the paid version of what they're already
engaging with for free.

## Scale channel risk-tolerance inversely with revenue concentration

Being cautious about investing heavily in a platform prone to account-
level risk (suspension, restriction, having to buy back access) is
rational while that platform would represent an outsized share of total
revenue — the downside of losing it is catastrophic. Once a diversified,
recurring revenue base exists elsewhere, the same platform-level risk
becomes more tolerable to lean into, because losing it stops being an
existential threat to the business. Revisit platform risk tolerance
periodically against current revenue concentration, rather than locking
in a risk stance formed when the business looked very different.
